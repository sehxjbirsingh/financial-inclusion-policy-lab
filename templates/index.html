METHODOLOGY & PIPELINE

"How We Did It": Step-by-Step Walkthrough

A transparent, step-by-step visual explanation of how raw World Bank survey microdata becomes meaningful pattern archetypes.

View Raw Pipeline JSON ↗
1

Step 1: Variable Curation & Missing Data Imputation

We started with 466 survey indicators in the World Bank Global Findex dataset. We curated 9 core indicators representing the 5 key pillars of financial inclusion (Access, Banking, Mobile, Savings, Credit, and Resilience).

The Missing Data Rule: Features with over 40% missingness are automatically dropped to prevent artificial bias. Remaining missing cells receive median imputation (filling gaps with the middle country's rate). Categorical fields (Region, Income Group) are kept purely for post-clustering analysis so the algorithm doesn't group countries solely by geography.
Overall Account (0% missing) Bank Account (0% missing) Emergency Resilience (1.3% missing) Mobile Money (39.2% missing) Savings Rate (29.4% missing)
2

Step 2: Dual Geometry Clustering (Euclidean vs Manhattan)

We tested cluster counts from K = 2 to K = 10. Standard Scikit-Learn KMeans only supports Euclidean distance. To honestly evaluate Manhattan distance, we implemented a true L1-Norm K-Medians algorithm using coordinate-wise medians.

How We Evaluated the Groups:
  • WCSS (Elbow Method): Measures how tightly packed each group is.
  • Silhouette Score: Measures how cleanly separated the groups are from each other (higher is better).
  • Davies-Bouldin Index: Measures overlap between groups (lower is better).
Recommended Setting: K = 4 balances mathematical cohesion with clear, practical policy meaning.
3

Step 3: Dimensionality Compression & Stability Auditing

To visualize 9 dimensions on a flat screen, we ran Principal Component Analysis (PCA). We also tested algorithmic stability across 10 different random seeds and 25 bootstrap resamplings.

Why Stability Matters: If an algorithm produces totally different country groups every time you run it, policymakers cannot trust it. Our model achieves a Mean Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) of 0.892, confirming that the groupings are robust. We also identify volatile "borderline countries" situated near cluster boundaries.
REFERENCE

Plain-English Glossary

Simple definitions written in everyday language (Grade 8 reading level) for every financial and data science term used in this lab.

Financial Inclusion

Ensuring that individuals and businesses have access to useful and affordable financial services—such as transactions, payments, savings, credit, and insurance—delivered in a responsible and sustainable way.

Cluster / Archetype

A group of countries that share very similar financial patterns (for example, high mobile money usage but low formal bank branch presence). Clustering finds natural peer groups without human bias.

K (Cluster Count)

The number of groups you instruct the computer algorithm to divide countries into. For example, K=4 means dividing the world's countries into 4 distinct pattern groups.

Mobile Money

A service that stores funds electronically on a mobile phone account, allowing users to send, receive, and store money without needing a traditional brick-and-mortar bank branch.

Financial Resilience

The ability of an ordinary household to withstand a sudden financial shock (like a hospital emergency or job loss) without having to sell off vital assets or borrow from high-interest loan sharks.

Silhouette Score

A number between -1.0 and +1.0 that grades how distinct and well-separated the groups are. Closer to +1.0 means the groups are clearly separated; closer to 0 means they blur together.

Davies-Bouldin Index

A mathematical score measuring similarity between clusters. Unlike Silhouette, lower is better. A lower number means clusters are tight internally and far apart from other groups.

WCSS (Within-Cluster Sum of Squares)

The total distance between countries and the center of their assigned group. When plotted against K, it forms an "elbow" curve helping identify the sweet spot where adding more groups stops adding value.

PCA (Principal Component Analysis)

A statistical technique that condenses 9 different financial indicators into 2 or 3 summary dimensions so we can visualize complex country patterns on a flat screen.

Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)

A score from 0 to 1 measuring how consistently countries get grouped together when the algorithm is run multiple times with different random starting seeds. Higher means more reliable.

Tiered e-KYC (Know Your Customer)

A regulation allowing low-income citizens to open basic entry-level bank accounts with just a phone number or national ID, without needing tax returns or formal proof of residence.

Chama / ROSCA / Tanda

Traditional informal community savings circles where friends or neighbors pool money each month and take turns receiving the lump sum payout.

About, Data Sources & Responsible Use

Project mission, official data source citations, and critical scientific limitations.

Data Source & Citation

All empirical data in this laboratory is sourced from the official World Bank Global Findex Database (2025 Release), the world's most comprehensive dataset on how adults around the globe save, borrow, make payments, and manage financial risk.

Demirgüç-Kunt, Asli, Leora Klapper, Dorothe Singer, and Saniya Ansar. The Global Findex Database: Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments, and Resilience. Washington, DC: World Bank.

Survey waves incorporated: 2011, 2014, 2017, 2021, and 2024. Download official microdata at worldbank.org/globalfindex ↗.

Critical Methodological Limitations

  • Exploratory, Not Prescriptive: Unsupervised clusters partition mathematical space into regions of proximity; they do not establish permanent developmental destinies, moral hierarchies, or official World Bank classifications.
  • Within-Country Inequalities: National aggregates inevitably hide severe localized disparities between wealthy urban centers and marginalized rural villages or refugee settlements.
  • Self-Reporting & Survey Intervals: Surveys are administered triennially with representative samples of ~1,000 respondents per nation; informal borrowing and cash savings are subject to self-reporting bias.
  • Correlation is Not Causation: An observed correlation between high mobile money penetration and emergency savings resilience does not prove that mobile money caused resilience. Both may be driven by general economic development.
